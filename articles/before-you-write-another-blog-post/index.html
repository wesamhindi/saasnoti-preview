Before you write another blog post, start here

Turn a real customer question into a focused article brief, with useful evidence and a clear next step.

Before opening a blank document, write down one question a potential customer has asked you. Choose a question that affects a decision: what something costs, which option fits, how a process works, or what to expect next.

That question is the starting point for your article. A keyword tool can help you investigate it, but you still need a clear reason for your business to answer it. Spend a little time on the brief before spending hours on the writing.

Give the page one job

Finish this sentence: After reading this page, someone will be able to… If you cannot finish it clearly, narrow the topic.

For example, a family photographer could help a client decide how long to book a session. That gives the article a concrete purpose: explain the choices, the tradeoffs, and the information needed to decide. A broad article about the importance of photography has a less obvious job.

Write down who the reader is, too. A parent booking a first family session needs a different explanation from a photographer comparing equipment. Choose one reader and use their questions to decide what belongs on the page.

Check whether the answer already exists

Look through your own site first. If a service page already addresses the question, improving it may be the better assignment. If the question deserves a separate guide, decide how that guide will support the service page.

Then read relevant search results. Note the choices readers face, the terms that need explaining, and the places where advice becomes vague. Treat this as research into the question, not an outline to copy. A useful brief should tell you what you can contribute beyond the existing answers.

Find something you can show

Collect a worked example, an original screenshot, a useful calculation, or a detail from your experience. Explain what it shows and where its limits are. Google's helpful-content guidance asks whether a page adds original information and demonstrates firsthand knowledge.

For the photographer, useful evidence could include an actual session timeline and the circumstances that affected it. If you do not have that evidence, use a clearly labeled hypothetical example. Never turn an invented scenario into a customer story.

Gather sources before asking an AI assistant to help write. Give it the brief and the material you have verified. Keep a separate list of claims still needing a source, including prices, statistics, and product instructions.

Copy this six-part brief

Here is a fictional example you can adapt to your own business:

  • Reader: A parent booking their first outdoor family photo session.
  • Question: How long should we book?
  • Useful answer: Explain how the number of people, location changes, and children's breaks affect the choice.
  • Evidence to gather: A real session timeline, the photographer's available packages, and any limits that change the recommendation.
  • Outside the scope: Camera settings, editing software, and a general history of portrait photography.
  • Next step: Read the family-session page or ask about the available booking options.

The evidence line is a reporting assignment. Fill it with real material before writing the finished article. If the answer still depends on a detail you do not know, name that detail and find it.

Give the reader a useful next step

Link to a service explanation, a pricing page, or a deeper guide where it helps someone continue. Make the link text describe the destination. Read the article once without its promotional sentences: does it still help the reader answer the original question?

For the final publishing checks, use our Squarespace blog SEO checklist. Keep content decisions and publishing settings on the same checklist so neither gets lost at the handoff.

Decide what you will measure

Record the publication date and the page's intended job. Later, use Search Console's Performance report to review queries, impressions, and clicks for the page. Track useful reader actions separately, such as a relevant inquiry.

Keep a note of substantive revisions and other changes to the site. Those notes give you context when reviewing performance. Start with a clear brief, publish something useful, and use what you learn to decide the next improvement.
