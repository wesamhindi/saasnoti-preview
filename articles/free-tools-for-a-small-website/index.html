Five free tools that help a small website make better decisions

What each tool actually tells you, where its evidence stops, and a simple way to turn reports into useful work.

A small website does not need a large software stack to start improving. It needs a way to find problems, decide which ones matter, and confirm that a change helped. These five tools cover different questions; none provides a complete judgment of your business or your content.

This is a researched selection based on official documentation and public tool pages checked on October 29, 2026. We did not run comparative product tests or connect SaaSNoti accounts for this guide. The suggested routines and examples are our editorial recommendations.

Use the page-audit worksheet to record the question, affected URL, evidence, proposed change, owner, and recheck date. A report earns its place when it changes a decision. Save screenshots or exports with the settings visible so a later comparison is meaningful.

1. Google Search Console: visibility and indexing

Search Console is free and reports on your presence in Google Search. It includes query and page performance, indexing diagnostics, and alerts. Registration is not a condition of appearing in Google, but access to your own property’s reports lets you investigate rather than guess.

Start with a verified property and a shortlist of pages you actually want discovered. URL Inspection includes indexed information and a live test. An indexing request does not guarantee inclusion.

Our first task: inspect the homepage, main service page, and one guide. Record their statuses before changing anything. If a guide is accessible but its indexed version is old, investigate the dates and crawl status. If Google chose a different canonical, understand that relationship before adding more pages.

Limit: this is evidence about Google. A good indexing status does not establish that the article is accurate, that the contact form works, or that visitors will become customers.

2. PageSpeed Insights: performance questions

PageSpeed Insights accepts a public page URL. Google lists it among its free speed-testing tools. Its documentation distinguishes simulated lab diagnostics from real-user field data collected over a preceding 28-day period. Small or new pages may lack enough field data; an origin-level result may appear instead.

Our first task: select a page that visitors use to decide or contact you. Record mobile and desktop results separately. Save the tested URL, date, and whether the field data describes the page or the whole origin. Pick one repeated, actionable issue that matches a visible user problem.

For example, if an enormous hero image appears to delay the main content, ask the implementer to reduce its delivery size while preserving a clear image. Then repeat the test under comparable conditions and use the page yourself. Treat this as a hypothesis to check, not a guaranteed diagnosis.

Limit: a lab score is not the experience of every visitor. Missing field data is not a failure, and a better score does not prove improved sales.

3. Bing Webmaster Tools: a second search view

Microsoft describes Bing Webmaster Tools as free. Its documented features include site verification, URL inspection, search performance, sitemap reporting, and technical scans. It also supports importing verified sites from Google Search Console.

Our first task: add the site through the official service, complete ownership verification, and inspect the same three pages you checked in Google. Keep a separate column for Bing’s observations. If one engine reports a fetch problem, investigate the exact URL and response rather than assuming that the other engine’s successful fetch settles it.

Use a technical scan as a list of candidate fixes. A duplicated description across several pages may deserve attention, but a working lead form usually deserves attention sooner than a cosmetic score. Assign priorities based on the reader’s task and the evidence.

Limit: Bing’s observations do not replace Google’s or measure every AI search service. The number of findings is not a reliable measure of business risk. Review the specific findings.

4. Rich Results Test: inspect supported markup

Google’s public Rich Results Test provides URL and code inputs without a paid subscription. It checks structured data relevant to supported Google rich results. Google’s policies require accurate markup; passing does not guarantee a rich result.

Our first task: test one representative article or product page after your template adds structured data. Read which item type was detected, then compare its name, author, price, or other relevant fields with what a visitor actually sees. Correct contradictory values at their source so future pages inherit the fix.

Do not invent reviews, ratings, or product offers to make a test look better. If the tool detects no supported item, first ask whether the page has a legitimate reason to use one. Not every page needs extra markup.

Limit: passing a machine check does not establish editorial truth or guarantee a search feature. A warning and an error also deserve different investigation; read the explanation before making a site-wide change.

5. Google Trends: relative interest and timing

Google’s training describes Trends Explore as free. The data FAQ explains that results use sampled searches normalized to a 0–100 scale. The numbers are relative interest, not monthly search volumes; low-volume terms can display zero.

Our first task: compare two plausible topic ideas using the same country, time period, search type, and category. Record whether each selection is a search term or a topic. Examine a longer period for recurring seasonality before interpreting a short spike as an enduring opportunity.

Suppose a business is deciding when to publish a preparation guide for a seasonal service. Use the observed pattern to propose a publication window, then check actual customer questions and your ability to help. This is a planning example; we are not claiming measured demand for a particular service.

Limit: relative interest does not tell you competition, purchase intent, profitability, or the exact number of people searching. Use the article planning checklist before turning a trend into a brief.

Turn the tools into a small routine

  1. At launch: verify search properties, inspect key pages, check the main page templates, and save a performance baseline.
  2. After an important change: revisit the tool that answers the original question. Confirm the changed page and the next action work on a phone.
  3. Monthly: review useful pages, new issues, and actual inquiries. Select one content improvement and one technical issue with clear evidence.

When a report is empty, write down the limitation. When two reports differ, compare what they measure before choosing a winner. These habits are more valuable than collecting screenshots of high scores. Keep the stack small enough that each report leads to an action you can explain.

What to take away

  • Choose a tool because it answers a specific question, not because it produces a score.
  • Keep indexing evidence, performance diagnostics, and search-interest estimates separate.
  • Save a baseline and change one identifiable thing before checking again.
