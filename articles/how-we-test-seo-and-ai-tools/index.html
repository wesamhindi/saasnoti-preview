How to evaluate an SEO or AI tool before you pay

Use one real task to compare the evidence, editing time, total cost, and practical fit of your next subscription.

Choose an SEO or AI tool by giving it a real job. Before comparing feature lists, write down the task that is taking too long, the result you need, and what you currently do to get there.

“Help me improve my website” is too broad to test. “Find a fixable issue on this service page and explain how to correct it” gives you something to inspect. So does “Turn these verified notes into an article brief without inventing evidence.”

Start with the bottleneck

Choose the part of your work that actually needs help. A tool that generates more text will be a poor fit if your real difficulty is finding reliable information or making time to publish.

  • Diagnosing a problem: Can the tool identify a specific issue, show its evidence, and explain the next action?
  • Preparing content: Can it use your source material accurately and produce a brief a writer can follow?
  • Editing a website: Can you inspect the proposed change, approve it, and recover the previous version?
  • Reviewing results: Can it answer your question about the page using data you can trace?

Pick one primary task for the first evaluation. Write down the must-have conditions before opening the product. This makes it harder for an impressive but irrelevant feature to decide the purchase.

Use the same small sample

Give each candidate the same page, source notes, and instructions. Use a copy of your material that you are comfortable sharing. Record the plan you tried, the date, and any permissions or setup required.

For a writing task, prepare a short source pack with a few verified facts and one clearly marked unknown. Ask for a brief and a list of unanswered questions. Check whether the output preserves the facts and leaves the unknown unresolved. An invented answer is a concrete problem you can record.

For an SEO task, choose a page you understand well. Ask the tool to explain its recommendation and point to the page detail or documentation supporting it. Keep a screenshot or export of the result so you can compare it with the other candidates.

Look for evidence you can inspect

Keep three labels in your notes: observed in your test, described by the vendor, and still unverified. A feature listed on a pricing page belongs in the second group until you try the version you intend to buy.

Use the same approach when reading reviews. Google's guidance for product reviews emphasizes evidence of experience, meaningful measurements, and benefits and drawbacks. Look for the sample task, the output, the tested plan, and the work needed to make the result usable.

A successful article-generation test tells you about producing that article. To judge search visibility or inquiries, you need observations of the published page over time. Decide which outcome matters for your purchase and what evidence would support it.

Count the work around the output

Time the whole task: setup, source preparation, waiting, corrections, export, and final checks. Record the usable result at the end. A fast first response can still leave you with a long editing job.

Alongside time, record the current subscription price and the usage allowance for the plan you need. Check whether seats, connected websites, exports, or additional usage change the cost. Use the provider's current pricing and billing documentation; save the checked date with your notes.

Compare that total with the amount of work you expect to use. If you publish occasionally, a larger allowance may have little practical value. If someone else will operate the tool, include their setup and review time in the decision.

Test the handoff and the exit

Before committing, try moving one result into the place where you will use it. For an article, that means checking headings, links, images, and formatting in your website editor. For a report, it means confirming that the export contains the detail you need.

  • Check what account access the tool requests and whether it can make changes.
  • Read the provider's explanation of how submitted content is stored and used, including any relevant account controls.
  • Confirm which work you can export and what happens to access after cancellation.
  • Find the renewal and cancellation settings before the trial ends.

These are checks to perform for each product and plan. Keep unanswered questions on the list rather than treating missing information as a pass.

Write a decision in plain language

Finish with a short decision record: “This tool is suitable for this task because we observed these results. It requires these corrections and this ongoing cost. We will reconsider if these conditions change.”

Keep the tool only if the tested improvement matters to your work. If the underlying problem is an unclear article assignment, start with our six-part content brief and evaluate the software against that.

What to take away

  • Define the task before comparing tools.
  • Ask what a claim is based on and what a result leaves out.
  • Test a small reversible workflow before committing.
