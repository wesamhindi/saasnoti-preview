Internal links for a small business site: a practical audit

Map useful reader journeys, repair missing connections, and write link labels that lead to the right answer.

An internal link should help someone take the next sensible step. A visitor reading about your process may need pricing. Someone comparing packages may need examples. Someone ready to enquire needs an obvious route to the right form.

This audit starts with those journeys. You will make a small page inventory, identify missing connections, improve confusing link labels, and check that the destinations work. You do not need to begin with an expensive crawler or an arbitrary target number of links.

Make a list of important destinations

Open your website editor and list the public pages a visitor should be able to find. Include the homepage, core services or products, useful guides, about page, and contact or booking page. Add a short purpose beside each address. Exclude private client material from the public-link exercise.

For each page, ask: Who needs this, what are they trying to decide, and where would they reasonably look for it? If you cannot explain the page's role, clarify its purpose before inventing links to support it.

Use three labels in your inventory:

  • Decision page: helps someone choose a service, product, or next action.
  • Explanation page: answers a question needed to make that decision well.
  • Evidence page: provides work samples, specifications, or another substantiated reason to trust the offer.

These are planning labels, not Google classifications. A page may serve more than one role. The purpose is to expose missing journeys, such as a detailed pricing guide that never leads to the service it discusses.

Draw a small map around one customer task

Google explains in its ecommerce structure guidance that navigation and links help it understand page relationships. That documentation addresses stores; the service-business map below is our practical adaptation, not a prescribed Google template.

Fictional example: a portrait studio wants an office manager to understand and book team headshots. Its pages might connect like this:

Starting pageNext destinationReason for the connection
Services overviewTeam headshotsChoose the service that matches the job.
Team headshotsPackage comparisonCompare the number of people, delivery, and included work.
Package comparisonReal team portfolioSee representative work before making contact.
Team headshotsOffice preparation checklistUnderstand what the team must prepare.
Preparation checklistTeam headshotsReturn to the service details when ready.
Team headshotsTeam enquiry formStart the relevant booking conversation.

The service page acts as an overview because it answers the main decision and points to deeper material. It does not need to contain every detail. The preparation checklist should still answer its own question before asking readers to book.

Repeat this exercise for another service only when that journey differs. Do not make a new overview page merely to create another layer. A small site can often use its existing service pages as the organizing structure.

Use an audit sheet that records the reader's need

Create one row for each connection you need to inspect or add. Start with the page audit worksheet and add the fields below.

  • Source URL: the page where the reader currently is.
  • Reader's next question: what they may need at this point.
  • Destination URL: the exact published address that answers it.
  • Current link text: what a reader can actually select.
  • Placement: menu, paragraph, comparison table, or related reading.
  • Observed problem: absent, misleading, broken, outdated, or unnecessary.
  • Action and owner: one concrete edit and who will make it.
  • Verified: date the published route was checked.

Begin with the pages most closely connected to enquiries, sales, or successful customer tasks. Open each starting page and try to complete the journey without using the editor. A route you can find only because you remember the address deserves a closer look.

Check that the link is a real route

Google's link guidance recommends anchors with usable href addresses and incoming links for important pages. It specifies no ideal numerical quota. A script-only click action is less reliable for crawling.

In a visual editor, use the normal link control and select the actual page. For a custom component, ask the developer to verify the rendered link. A button-shaped design is fine for navigation when its underlying implementation is appropriate; appearance alone cannot settle this check.

Record any route that ends on an error, a different service, or an unexpected login. If a destination was renamed, update the source link to the current address. Google's canonical guidance recommends linking consistently to the preferred version. Use your inventory to avoid mixing old and new addresses.

Make the promise clear before the click

The W3C's link-purpose guidance explains that readers should be able to determine a link's purpose from its text or associated context. This is particularly useful when navigating with assistive technology.

Compare these invented edits:

Unclear labelMore useful labelWhat improved
Find out moreCompare team headshot packagesThe destination's task is explicit.
Our guidePrepare your office for photographyThe reader knows what help to expect.
Book nowAsk about team availabilityThe wording matches an enquiry form rather than promising a completed booking.

Choose wording that the destination actually fulfills. If the page only offers a request form, do not imply instant confirmation. If you link a downloadable checklist, make that format apparent. Ask a colleague to predict what each link opens before selecting it; mismatches reveal wording you can fix.

Keep the surrounding paragraph useful. A service explanation should not become a string of highlighted phrases. If two links interrupt the same sentence, consider whether the second belongs after the explanation or in a short related-reading list.

Use Search Console as a clue, then check the site

In the Links report, inspect internal links and select an important destination to see listed source pages. The report is a sample, can include previously found links, and is not a complete current inventory. Treat an absent entry as a prompt to investigate, not proof that a page has no incoming links.

Compare the report with your manual map. If the homepage dominates while an essential service seems hard to reach, walk through the relevant menu and article routes. If the page still has search inclusion problems after fixing obvious routes, use the indexing diagnostic workflow.

Finish with a visitor test

  1. Start from the homepage and from one useful article. Complete the intended journey in each case.
  2. Check the published site on a phone as well as a larger screen. Confirm that the menu exposes the route and the link is practical to select.
  3. Use the keyboard to move through links. Confirm that the active location is visible and the destination is understandable.
  4. Check each edited destination and record the result in the sheet.
  5. When publishing another article, identify both where it should lead and which existing page should introduce it.

Review this map when a service changes, a page moves, or your content review reveals a new reader need. Measure completion by whether the route works and helps the reader. More internal links alone are not evidence of a better website.

What to take away

  • Build links around a reader's next question and the actual purpose of each destination.
  • Check crawlable destinations, clear labels, and published routes instead of chasing a link-count target.
  • Use Search Console as supporting evidence; its Links report is not a complete current inventory.
